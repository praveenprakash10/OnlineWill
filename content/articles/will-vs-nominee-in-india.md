A nominee is often treated as the owner of an asset. That is usually incorrect. In many cases, a nominee is a caretaker who receives the asset so it can be passed to the rightful heirs.

## Nominee

A **nominee** is named on a specific product—bank account, insurance policy, mutual fund, demat account, and so on. Their role depends on the product and applicable law, but nomination alone rarely replaces a will. A nomination is a mechanism that permits an institution to lawfully hand over an asset to the named nominee and obtain a valid discharge for that payment in event of the death of the investor/asset owner. It generally does not decide who beneficially owns the asset.

## Legal Heir / Beneficiary under a will

A **will** names who should ultimately receive your estate. When drafted and executed correctly, it is one of the strongest ways to express your wishes across assets. A will is a testamentary instrument that disposes of a person’s estate and identifies the beneficiaries who are legally entitled to inherit.

A Will is a legal declaration where you define:

* Who inherits your assets
* How they’re divided
* Who executes your wishes (the executor)

It **overrides nominations**, unless the Will explicitly confirms the nominee as the beneficiary.

## What Happens if You Only Nominate and Don’t Make a Will?

* Your nominee receives the asset
* Legal heirs (like spouse, children) can challenge the nominee’s right
* Disputes may lead to freezing of accounts, court cases, and delays

## Practical takeaway

1. Make a **clear, detailed Will** covering all your assets
2. Keep **nominations updated on financial products** for smoother transfers.
3. **Align your nominations** with your Will (to avoid contradiction)
4. **Update both regularly** after major life events (marriage, birth, separation)
